# Procedural Rhetoric (Bogost, *Persuasive Games*, 2007)

**Core claim:** games make arguments through their **rules and processes** — through
what the system lets you do, rewards, and punishes — not through words, images, or
cutscenes. Bogost calls this *procedural rhetoric*: persuasion through rule-based
representations and interactions. **The model you play IS the message.**

This is the most important lens for a serious game, because **no usability or
playability heuristic can detect a rule system that teaches the wrong thing.** A game
can pass every PLAY heuristic and still argue something its designers never intended.

## Worked examples

- **Spent** argues poverty is a trap by making every choice cost something you can't
  spare — you feel the squeeze procedurally, not because text tells you.
- **The McDonald's Videogame** argues industrial food requires corruption: to stay
  solvent you must deforest and bribe. The lesson is enforced by the rules.

## The audit (do this for every serious game)

1. **State the intended argument.** What should the rules make the player conclude or
   feel? (Often hidden in the intro copy — quote it.)
2. **Derive the actual argument from incentives alone.** Cover the text and art. Look
   only at: what increases the score / advances you / wins, and what loses. Ask:
   - **What is the dominant strategy?** The optimal way to play given the math.
   - **What does winning that way teach?** That behavior is the game's real thesis.
3. **Compare.** If the dominant strategy enacts the intended lesson → the rhetoric is
   sound. If a player can win by doing the OPPOSITE of the learning goal → the
   procedural argument is broken, regardless of how good the writing is.

## Common failure modes

- **Toothless virtue:** the "right" behavior (safety, help-seeking, learning) earns a
  resource that has no bearing on winning → the game says it's optional.
- **Rewarded vice:** the behavior the game warns against is also the most efficient way
  to score → the game trains it while claiming to discourage it.
- **Single dominant resource:** one resource has real stakes (a fail state) and the
  rest are cosmetic → the game's actual argument collapses to "optimize that one
  thing," erasing the intended tradeoff.
- **No real choice:** every scenario has an obviously correct option → it's a quiz with
  a story skin; it asserts a lesson rather than letting the player discover it.

## Why it's usually the top finding

A broken procedural argument defeats the entire purpose of building a game (rather than
writing a handout): the interactivity is teaching the wrong thing, persuasively. Rate
these failures high severity (typically 3–4) and recommend fixing the **incentive
math** — make the intended behavior the winning behavior — before any art or polish.
