# MDA Framework (Hunicke, LeBlanc & Zubek, 2004)

MDA = **Mechanics → Dynamics → Aesthetics**. Designers build left to right; players
experience right to left. For evaluation, the power move is to reason **backward**.

- **Mechanics** — the rules and data: the resources, the choices, the point/HP deltas,
  the win/lose conditions.
- **Dynamics** — the run-time behavior that emerges when players act on the mechanics
  over time: the strategies, tensions, and rhythms that actually show up in play.
- **Aesthetics** — the desired emotional responses: e.g., challenge, fellowship,
  discovery, expression, "fantasy," narrative tension. For serious games add the
  intended **realization** (the insight the player should leave with).

## How to use it in an evaluation

1. Name the **intended Aesthetic** — the feeling/insight the game is going for. For a
   clerkship sim that might be "feel the weight of competing priorities and realize
   that protecting wellbeing and learning are both at stake."
2. Identify the **Dynamics** that would produce that feeling — e.g., every choice
   forces a real tradeoff with no free lunch; scarcity creates pressure; consequences
   compound.
3. Inspect the **Mechanics** and ask whether they actually generate those dynamics. A
   tradeoff aesthetic requires that no single resource dominates and that ignoring a
   resource has real cost. If one resource has a hard fail state and the rest have no
   stakes, the dynamic is "protect the one resource," and the intended aesthetic of
   balance never emerges.

## The common serious-game failure: MDA mismatch

The designer specifies an Aesthetic ("hard moral tradeoffs," "empathy," "urgency") but
the Mechanics produce a different Dynamic ("obvious right answer," "grind," "wait").
When you find a mismatch, state all three layers explicitly:

> Intended aesthetic: X. Mechanics as built: Y. Resulting dynamic: Z, which produces
> [different feeling], not X. Fix the mechanic (not the art) to restore the dynamic.

This framing tells the team to fix the **rules**, which is cheap and structural,
rather than papering over a systemic problem with content or polish.
